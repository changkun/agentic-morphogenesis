\section{The Ralph Experiment: Convergent Evolution Without Specification}

The Wallfacer experiment demonstrates bottleneck migration and role fission within a single pipeline. A natural follow-up question is whether multiple independent instances exhibit convergent or divergent evolutionary trajectories, and what factors govern the balance between the two. To investigate this, we conducted a second experiment using a simpler pipeline that lacks Wallfacer's role differentiation.

\subsection{Setup}

We used \emph{ralph}, an autonomous agent pipeline that runs a three-phase loop: a \textbf{Thinker} analyzes the current project state and proposes exactly one goal for the next round; a \textbf{Worker} implements that goal; and a \textbf{Committer} stages all changes and pushes a commit. Unlike Wallfacer's multi-role architecture, ralph's loop is fixed: no Cartographer, no Critic, no fission. The pipeline persists execution history as JSON logs, enabling the Thinker to condition each new goal on the full trajectory of prior rounds.

We pointed ralph at three empty Git repositories with no seed task and no specification of what to build. All three instances used the same LLM substrate (Claude) and the same three-phase loop structure. Two instances (Sandbox and Simulator) used the stock Thinker prompt, which provides no strategic guidance beyond a generic instruction to ``be creative.'' The third instance (Explorer) used a modified Thinker prompt that included two additional signals: awareness of its current round number, and an \emph{explore/exploit score}, a deterministic sinusoidal function of the round number with period ${\approx}6$ rounds and ${\pm}0.2$ random jitter, presented to the Thinker as a percentage (e.g., \texttt{explore=73\%}) with one of three steering phrases (``lean toward exploration,'' ``lean toward exploitation,'' or ``balance''). The signal is not adaptive or learned; it is a fixed oscillation that periodically nudges the Thinker toward deepening existing capabilities rather than adding new ones. This 2+1 design was not planned as a controlled experiment; the prompt modification preceded the experiment and the divergent trajectories were observed post hoc. Nevertheless, the design affords a natural comparison: the two stock-prompt instances serve as near-replications of each other, while the modified instance isolates the effect of a single prompt-level variable. Two instances completed 42 rounds and one completed 33 rounds before being stopped. Table~\ref{tab:ralph_repos} summarizes the resulting codebases.

The resulting evidence is stronger than a single anecdote but still far from a fully powered experiment. Three runs are enough to surface an interesting pattern; they are not enough to cleanly separate prompt effects, sampling variance, and latent substrate priors.

\subsection{Observations}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/ralph_loc_growth.pdf}
\caption{Cumulative lines of code across three repositories initialized from empty directories with no seed task. All three converged on cellular automaton implementations. Growth is monotonically increasing with near-zero churn (3.9\%, 0.5\%, and 3.0\% deletion ratios respectively).}
\label{fig:ralph_loc}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observation 4: Convergent goal selection from null initial conditions.} All three instances independently chose to build a cellular automaton simulator as their first goal and continued developing it for every subsequent round. This convergence occurred without any seed task, without inter-instance communication, and despite the prompt-level difference between the Explorer and the other two instances: neither the explore/exploit signal nor its absence influenced the initial domain choice. The ``null'' initial condition is, of course, not truly null: it includes the LLM's entire training distribution, which implicitly encodes priors over what constitutes an interesting, tractable, and self-contained software project. The convergence on cellular automata suggests that this class of programs occupies a strong attractor basin in the LLM's prior over generative tasks, likely because cellular automata are well-represented in training data, produce visually compelling output, and admit incremental extension without architectural refactoring. This parallels convergent evolution in biology, where independent lineages arrive at similar solutions (e.g., camera eyes evolving independently over 40 times \cite{land2002}) when facing analogous selection pressures.

\textbf{Observation 5: Divergent specialization along orthogonal axes.} Despite convergent initial goals, the three codebases specialized in qualitatively different directions (Figure~\ref{fig:ralph_features}). Explorer, written in C, developed deep scientific analysis capabilities: 15+ information-theoretic overlays (Shannon entropy, Lyapunov exponents, transfer entropy, Wolfram classification), multi-rule zones, wormhole portals, and exotic topologies (torus, Klein bottle, M\"obius strip). Sandbox pursued breadth, accumulating 27 interactive simulation modes spanning five categories (cellular automata, physics, biology, procedural generation, algorithms). Simulator combined algorithmic diversity with tooling: 28+ simulation modes alongside genetic algorithm rule discovery, headless batch rendering, and GIF/PNG export.

The Simulator's mode count (131) dwarfs the others, reflecting its strategy of adding many small simulation variants.}

Figure~\ref{fig:ralph_metrics} quantifies this divergence. The Explorer's mean cyclomatic complexity (22.9) is over three times that of the Sandbox (6.8), reflecting both the inherent verbosity of C and the depth of its analysis routines. The Simulator accumulated the most simulation modes (131) despite having the fewest rounds (33), suggesting a strategy of adding many small, self-contained variants rather than deepening existing ones.

The 2+1 experimental design allows us to partially disentangle stochastic from prompt-driven divergence. The two stock-prompt instances (Sandbox and Simulator) followed near-parallel trajectories: both defaulted to a breadth-first strategy of adding a new simulation mode each round, and they share 15 of their modes (including Lenia, Wa-Tor, Boids, Lattice Boltzmann, Ising model, and Wave Function Collapse). The Explorer, whose Thinker was periodically nudged toward exploitation, followed a qualitatively different depth-first trajectory: it stayed with one simulation model (Game of Life) and layered increasingly sophisticated analysis on top of it (Shannon entropy, Lyapunov maps, Fourier spectra, topological Betti numbers, renormalization group flow). The near-replication between Sandbox and Simulator, contrasted with the Explorer's divergence, suggests that the explore/exploit signal, a single scalar variable in the Thinker prompt, is the primary driver of the depth-versus-breadth split, rather than stochastic variation in LLM sampling.

This finding has two implications. First, a minimal prompt-level intervention can redirect an autonomous system's entire developmental trajectory, suggesting that the explore/exploit balance is a potent control variable for agentic morphogenesis. Second, the near-identical trajectories of the two stock-prompt instances provide evidence that the LLM's implicit prior over generative tasks is not merely an attractor for initial goal selection (Observation~4) but continues to constrain the full developmental trajectory when no countervailing signal is applied. The pattern of convergence-then-divergence is consistent with adaptive radiation in evolutionary biology \cite{schluter2000}: a population that enters a new ecological zone (here, the ``cellular automaton niche'') initially exploits the same resources before diverging into specialized sub-niches, but the degree of divergence is modulated by a selection pressure (the explore/exploit signal) that has no direct biological analogue.

\textbf{Observation 6: Monotonic growth without self-initiated refactoring.} Figure~\ref{fig:ralph_loc} shows that all three codebases grew monotonically, with deletion ratios below 4\%. None of the three instances ever proposed refactoring its own codebase, in contrast with the Wallfacer experiment's Observation~2, where the three-agent configuration proposed refactoring at approximately 51{,}000 lines. We interpret this as negative evidence supporting the fission principle: ralph's fixed thinker-worker-committer loop lacks a Cartographer role that would produce the structured self-representation needed for architectural self-awareness. Without a legible abstraction of global system state, the Thinker's planning horizon remains local: it can propose the next feature but cannot diagnose structural debt. This suggests that self-diagnostic capability is an emergent property of role differentiation, not an intrinsic capability of the underlying LLM.
