\section{Related Work}

The paper sits at the intersection of four literatures: LLM-based agent systems, self-adaptive software architectures, preference-based oversight and optimization, and older organizational or cybernetic theories of regulation.

\subsection{LLM Agents and Agentic Software Engineering}

Recent LLM-agent work has established a broad design space for role decomposition, memory, reflection, and tool use. Single-agent systems such as Reflexion \cite{shinn2023reflexion} and Voyager \cite{wang2023voyager} emphasize verbal self-improvement or open-ended skill acquisition. Multi-agent systems such as CAMEL \cite{li2023camel}, AutoGen \cite{wu2024autogen}, MetaGPT \cite{hong2023metagpt}, ChatDev \cite{qian2024chatdev}, and Magentic-One \cite{fourney2024magenticone} study role assignment, communication protocols, and orchestration patterns. In software engineering specifically, SWE-agent \cite{yang2024sweagent} demonstrates that interface design between the agent and the repository environment is a first-order determinant of performance.

Our work differs from this literature in two respects. First, the dependent variable is not benchmark success under a fixed architecture, but endogenous architectural change under sustained operation. Second, the empirical setting is open-ended rather than fully task-specified: Ralph instances begin from empty repositories with no seed task, and Wallfacer is allowed to adapt its own role structure over time. The closest overlap is therefore not with benchmark-driven comparisons alone, but with work on open-ended agent trajectories and long-horizon systems behavior.

\subsection{Self-Adaptive and Autonomic Software Systems}

The most direct systems lineage is autonomic and self-adaptive software. Kephart and Chess's vision of autonomic computing \cite{kephart2003} and architecture-based self-adaptation frameworks such as Rainbow \cite{garlan2004,garlan2009} treat monitoring, diagnosis, planning, and execution as explicit feedback loops over a software architecture model. Brun et al.\ \cite{brun2009} frame self-adaptive systems as systems that modify behavior in response to internal and external change, while preserving higher-level goals.

The present paper can be read as an agentic extension of that tradition. The proposed Cartographer resembles an evolving architecture knowledge model; the conjectured Coordinator and Monitor resemble specialized control-loop components; and the Architect role generalizes a MAPE-K style controller to a setting in which the decision-making elements are themselves LLM agents that can emit proposals, summaries, and preference judgments. The main difference is that the adaptive substrate is no longer a conventional controller but an organization of stochastic language agents with limited context windows and high communication costs.

\subsection{Preference Learning, Abstention, and Human Oversight}

The formal core of the paper builds on Gaussian Process preference learning \cite{chu2005}, active preference learning \cite{houlsby2011}, and preferential Bayesian optimization \cite{gonzalez2017}. These methods replace absolute reward values with ordinal comparisons, which is attractive when stakeholders can state which outcome they prefer but cannot assign reliable scalar scores. In online settings, the same family of ideas connects to contextual dueling bandits \cite{dudik2015}, where the system learns from pairwise comparisons conditioned on context.

Our Trust GP also relates to the literature on \emph{abstention} or \emph{reject-option} learning \cite{bartlett2008}. The practical question is not only which action is best, but when the system should decline autonomy and escalate to a human. Relative to prior work, the contribution here is to treat the human-oversight boundary itself as a learned, context-dependent decision surface over architectural state, workload, and uncertainty, rather than as a static hand-authored rule.

\subsection{Organizational, Cybernetic, and Security Antecedents}

The role-fission argument draws on organizational design and coordination theory. Simon's near-decomposability \cite{simon1962}, Mintzberg's structural configurations \cite{mintzberg1979}, and Malone and Crowston's coordination theory \cite{malone1994} all explain why differentiation can improve local performance while creating new coordination burdens. Brooks' communication-cost argument \cite{brooks1975} and Conway's Law \cite{conway1968} provide adjacent intuitions about when additional structure helps and when it becomes a liability.

The bottleneck-migration perspective is also shaped by cybernetics and security. Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety \cite{ashby1956} and the Good Regulator theorem \cite{conant1970} motivate the idea that stronger regulation requires richer internal models, while Thompson's ``trusting trust'' attack \cite{thompson1984}, Trusted Computing Base reasoning \cite{rushby1981}, Byzantine agreement \cite{lamport1982}, and Zero Trust Architecture \cite{rose2020} show how verification and trust allocation remain structural problems even in engineered systems. These works do not directly study LLM-agent organizations, but they supply the conceptual language for understanding why verification pressures can produce architectural differentiation rather than disappear.

Finally, the paper's more speculative sections draw on second-order cybernetics, autopoiesis, and self-reference \cite{maturana1980,vonfoerster2003,lawvere1969,godel1931}. We keep those connections, but treat them as conceptual extensions rather than part of the paper's empirical core. That distinction matters: the scientific contribution of the present draft is stronger when these ideas are positioned as hypotheses and interpretive lenses, not as already-validated explanations.
